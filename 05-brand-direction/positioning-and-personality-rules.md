# Positioning and Personality Rules

This file defines how to determine brand positioning, identify and validate differentiators, define brand personality and register, and determine what the brand must not feel like.

---

## Part 1: Positioning

### What positioning means here

Positioning answers the question: *In the customer's mind, what kind of business is this, and why is it the right choice?*

Strong positioning is:
- Specific enough to exclude some customers (not right for everyone)
- Based on something the business can actually prove or consistently deliver
- Different enough from competitors to be meaningful
- Connected to something the audience genuinely cares about

Weak positioning is:
- "The best in [city]" — unverifiable superlative
- "Quality service" — every competitor says this
- "We care about our customers" — table stakes, not a position
- "Your local [business type]" — geographic alone is not a position

### Positioning construction process

Build the positioning statement from these components:

1. **Audience:** Who specifically?
2. **Category:** What type of service/product are they providing?
3. **Key differentiator:** What makes this business meaningfully different?
4. **Proof anchor:** What makes the differentiator believable?

Draft structure:
> For [audience], [business name] is the [category] that [differentiator]. Unlike [typical alternative], [business name] [proof anchor or key claim].

This is for internal brand clarity — not necessarily final copy. But if the statement cannot be completed without generic language, the positioning is not strong enough yet.

### Challenging weak positioning

When prior inputs only offer generic claims:
- Ask: What specifically do the existing customers value most about this business?
- Ask: What problem does this business solve that most alternatives in the category don't?
- Ask: What proof exists that competitors cannot claim?

If no differentiator can be found even after this process, that is a significant brand risk. Flag it. Note that the brand will need to lean harder on proof density, founder story, and operational trust signals to compensate.

## Part 2: Differentiators

### Differentiator categories

**Type 1 — Process-based:** The business does things differently (safer technique, faster turnaround, more thorough process, custom approach).

**Type 2 — Proof-based:** The business has more or better evidence than competitors (reviews, results, certifications, before/after, client list).

**Type 3 — Model-based:** The business is structured differently (dedicated stylist, same-day availability, flat-rate pricing, no contracts, guarantee).

**Type 4 — Niche-based:** The business serves a specific segment better than generalists do (serves only high-end residential, specializes in one service, works only with specific industry).

**Type 5 — Story-based:** The business has an origin, founder, or local story that creates authentic trust (founder spent 20 years in the industry, family business, started in this neighborhood, built around a personal belief).

### Differentiator validation rules

A differentiator is usable if:
- The business can prove it (evidence exists or can be demonstrated)
- A typical competitor cannot immediately claim the same thing
- The audience actually cares about it in their decision-making

A differentiator is NOT usable if:
- It is an industry table stake (every legitimate business in the category can claim it)
- It cannot be proven or shown in any way
- The audience doesn't factor it into their decision

If a differentiator is story-based or process-based without proof, flag it. It can be used, but it should be reinforced with other signals.

## Part 3: Brand Register Spectrum

Place the brand on this spectrum before defining personality and tone. Every personality and tone decision should be calibrated to this register.

```
← APPROACHABLE ────────────────────────────────── AUTHORITATIVE →
Personal/warm    Friendly expert    Confident professional    Institutional/formal
     ↕                  ↕                    ↕                        ↕
Conversational      Direct + warm        Clear + confident       Structured + formal
Story-led           Proof + warmth       Evidence + polish       Credentials + restraint
Local feel          Community trust      Category expert         Industry authority
```

**Placement rule:** The register should match what the audience needs to feel confident making a decision. If the audience is anxious or skeptical (e.g., they've been burned before), warmer and more personal is stronger. If the audience is analytical and comparison-shopping (e.g., B2B buyers), more authoritative and evidence-forward is stronger.

Mismatches to flag:
- A local one-person cleaning service positioned as an "institutional authority" — feels fake
- A high-end medical specialist using casual, warm-neighbor language — feels imprecise and reduces confidence
- A B2B consultant sounding like a life coach — confuses the buyer about what they're actually buying

## Part 4: Brand Personality

Define the brand personality across five dimensions. Each dimension should be placed on a spectrum with a specific description — not just a label.

### Dimension 1: Confidence level

```
← Humble/deferential ─────── Assured/confident ─────── Bold/assertive →
```

Most local service businesses sit 60–70% toward confident. Consultants and agencies may sit further right. Medical and wellness may sit more toward assured/measured.

### Dimension 2: Warmth level

```
← Cool/clinical ─────────── Warm/approachable ─────────── Familiar/casual →
```

High-trust local services (plumbing, home services, personal training) should lean warmer. Premium professional services should stay in the middle. Pure credential-authority brands (surgeons, financial advisors) may lean cooler.

### Dimension 3: Formality level

```
← Very casual ───────────── Conversational ────────── Professional ────── Formal →
```

Most local service websites: conversational to professional. Enterprise/legal/financial: formal.

### Dimension 4: Energy level

```
← Calm/steady ───────── Purposeful/focused ─────── Energetic/dynamic →
```

Energy should match the audience's emotional state. Anxious audience = calmer energy. Motivated/aspirational audience = higher energy. Sophisticated audience = controlled energy.

### Dimension 5: Authority level

```
← Peer-level ─────────── Expert ─────────── Authority ─────────── Institutional →
```

For most local businesses: expert register. For specialists with significant credentials: authority register. For large agencies or established firms: authority to institutional.

## Part 5: Tone Guidance

Tone guidance is strategic. It defines how the brand should sound — not specific words or final sentences. That belongs to copy stages.

### Define these four tone attributes:

**What the tone IS:**
[3–4 adjectives that are specific, not generic. Back each with a behavioral description.]
Example: "Direct — says what it means without softening language to avoid commitment."

**What the tone IS NOT:**
[3–4 specific anti-patterns for this brand.]
Example: "Not salesy — no pressure language, no urgency manipulation, no 'limited time only.'"

**How the brand speaks about the customer:**
[Does it treat them as intelligent adults making a real decision? Worried homeowners who need reassurance? Ambitious professionals who need a partner?]

**How the brand speaks about itself:**
[Does it lead with what it does or how it does it? Does it make claims or demonstrate? Does it express confidence or invite trust?]

### Tone rules for specific situations:

- **Proof statements:** [e.g., "State the fact, let it speak — no over-qualifying or false modesty."]
- **CTAs:** [e.g., "Direct action language — not 'reach out to learn more' but 'get a free quote today.'"]
- **Explanations:** [e.g., "Plain language — no jargon unless the audience uses and values it."]
- **Trust building:** [e.g., "Show, don't claim — lead with reviews and results, not assertions about quality."]

## Part 6: What the Brand Must Not Feel Like

Define 4–6 specific anti-identities with explanations of why each is dangerous for this specific business.

**Format for each entry:**
> **Must not feel like [X]:** [Why this would hurt this business specifically — not a generic reason].

### Common anti-identities by business type:

**Home services:**
- Must not feel like a corporate chain — local trust depends on human connection
- Must not feel like a cheap discount service — undercuts the quality positioning
- Must not feel like an AI-generated template site — destroys local authenticity

**Medical/wellness:**
- Must not feel like a pushy sales machine — the audience is already skeptical of upselling
- Must not feel overly clinical and cold — it reduces approachability and appointment bookings
- Must not feel like a spa cliché — undercuts the medical credibility

**Consultant/professional services:**
- Must not feel like a life coach — confuses the buyer about the type of work being offered
- Must not feel like a startup pitch — the audience wants stability and expertise, not disruption narrative
- Must not feel like a corporate brochure — too generic to be memorable or trusted

**Salon/beauty:**
- Must not feel like a generic spa template — over-saturated aesthetic category
- Must not feel cheap or discount — signals quality mismatch
- Must not feel inaccessible or intimidating — the core booking action needs to feel easy
