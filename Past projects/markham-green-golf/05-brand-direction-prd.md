## 1. Project Overview

Markham Green Golf Club is a public 9-hole golf course in Markham, Ontario that must navigate a specific brand challenge: it needs to feel like a proper, credible golf club — not a beaten-up municipal track — without triggering the "exclusive, intimidating, not for me" response that pushes casual golfers toward the couch instead of the course. The primary audience of recreational Markham-area golfers aged 25–60 has actively been repelled by formal private clubs; they are price-sensitive and want to feel welcome, not judged. Every brand decision must hold the tension between "real golf" (credibility) and "no pretension" (accessibility) — and never sacrifice either side.

---

## 2. Brand Direction Summary

**Brand in one sentence:** "The neighbourhood golf course that takes the game seriously but not itself."

**Primary perception goal:** The audience should believe Markham Green is a legitimate, well-maintained golf course managed by real professionals — not a neglected park with flags stuck in the ground.

**Primary emotional goal:** The audience should feel welcomed and at ease — confident that they belong here and won't be judged for their handicap, their clothes, or how often they play.

**Brand register:** 7/10 toward authoritative. Serif headings and dark green palette signal a proper golf club. Warm photography and direct copy ensure it never tips into exclusivity. The aesthetic earns credibility first, then uses tone to undo the intimidation that credibility risks creating.

**Key brand constraint:** The brand must never feel elite or exclusive. The moment Markham Green looks like it costs $150 to play or requires a membership, the primary audience bounces. $44 green fees and a PGA Professional must coexist in the same brand system — quality without gatekeeping.

## 5. Brand Promise

> "A real round of golf in Markham — affordable, accessible, and genuinely welcoming."

**Why this promise:** The audience has been priced out of or made unwelcome at every other option. Angus Glen costs $100+. Private clubs have dress codes and etiquette pressure. Buttonville is a municipal track without professional instruction. Markham Green can make a promise that is simultaneously more credible than Buttonville and more welcoming than Angus Glen — and it can keep that promise because the combination of PGA Professional management + $44 green fees + community feel is real and verifiable.

**What breaks this promise:** Staff who are unfriendly at the pro shop, a course in visibly poor condition, unclear pricing, or a website that looks expensive and formal. On the website specifically: any design element that signals "members only," "application required," or "not for beginners" breaks the promise before the visitor even reads the pricing.

## 6. Positioning

**Positioning statement (internal use):**
> For recreational golfers in Markham and York Region, Markham Green Golf Club is the public golf course that delivers a real, maintained course with professional instruction at a price that doesn't require a special occasion. Unlike Angus Glen (inaccessible on price) and Buttonville (accessible but impersonal), Markham Green combines PGA Professional management with genuine community feel and green fees under $50.

**Positioning rationale:** This positioning wins on the "accessible professional golf" gap in Markham. There is no other course that combines: (a) PGA of Canada Professional management, (b) public access with no membership requirement, (c) green fees under $50, and (d) an on-site professional instruction program. The brand strategy is to make all four of these coexist visibly — not to hide the price, not to over-emphasize the prestige, but to make both facts feel normal and correct together.

**Where the brand sits vs. competitors:**
- Positioned above: Buttonville Municipal Golf Course — Markham Green is more professionally managed and more welcoming; it should feel like a real golf club, not a city park with flags
- Positioned against: The "expensive and hard to get on" perception of Angus Glen and similar prestige courses — Markham Green is the alternative for golfers who want the experience without the barrier
- NOT positioned against: Private club members who prioritize prestige, competitive golfers who want challenge and difficulty ratings — these audiences are not the target

## 7. Differentiators

**Primary differentiator (the one thing that matters most):**
> PGA of Canada Professional management at a public course with green fees under $50 — the only Markham course where you get professional-grade oversight at casual-golfer pricing.

**How it is proven:** Scott Haynes' PGA of Canada Professional credential is verifiable and named. The green fees are published and specific. The combination is unusual enough that stating both together is itself proof.

**Supporting differentiators:**
1. Salazar Golf Academy on-site instruction — type: niche — proof: Greg Salazar is named, credentialed, with a confirmed curriculum (Ladies Learn to Golf, Junior Camps, Private Lessons)
2. Community feel — regulars know each other, staff knows members by name — type: story — proof: this is the client's own description of the atmosphere; it is authentic, not manufactured
3. Easy access, no advance booking drama — type: process — proof: no booking windows fill weeks in advance; the course is genuinely accessible

**Differentiators explicitly rejected:**
- "We have the best course in Markham" — rejected because prestige competition is not winnable at this price point and would alienate the accessibility-seeking audience
- "Golf for everyone" — rejected because it is a generic, unprovenanced claim that any course can make and that says nothing specific

## 9. Emotional Impression Goals

**Primary emotion to create:** Ease — the visitor should feel that playing here would be uncomplicated, enjoyable, and appropriate for who they are as a golfer.

**Secondary emotion:** Quiet confidence — the sense that this is a real, quality course, not a compromise. The audience is not looking for the cheapest possible golf; they want good value that doesn't feel cheap. The brand should make the $44 green fee feel like a good deal at a good course, not a signal of low quality.

**Emotion to avoid:** Intimidation — any suggestion of membership requirements, dress codes, advanced etiquette, or prestige hierarchy will cause the primary audience to self-select out. The brand must actively prevent this response.

**Emotion gap:** Without strong photography and clear PGA credential visibility, the site risks feeling like a generic local business — generating "looks fine I guess" rather than "this is exactly what I want." The brand must create genuine positive feeling, not just neutral adequacy.

## 10. Trust Strategy

**How trust is earned for this specific audience:** Casual recreational golfers are not skeptical about quality in the same way premium service buyers are. They are skeptical about: (a) is this course actually maintained and playable? (b) will I feel welcome or out of place? (c) is the price transparent and fair? The trust strategy addresses all three: photography handles (a), community-feel copy handles (b), and published pricing handles (c).

**Trust sequence:**
1. Photography — the hero course photograph is the first trust signal. A quality drone shot of a well-maintained green answers "is this a real, playable course?" faster than any copy.
2. PGA credentials + Google rating — the trust bar immediately below the hero answers "who runs this and are others happy with it?" Named, credentialed professionals + social proof.
3. Transparent pricing — the pricing table on the homepage (condensed) and `/course` (full) answers "will I feel ambushed by the price?" Visible, specific pricing is itself a trust signal for this audience.

**Trust anchor:** Course photography. If the photography is strong, it creates an immediate positive impression that credentials and pricing can then reinforce. If the photography is weak, no amount of credential display or pricing transparency can compensate for the visual doubt it creates.

## 13. Tone Guidance

**The tone IS:**
- Direct: Prices are stated plainly. No "starting from" hedging. No "contact us for availability."
- Welcoming: Copy consistently signals that the course is for everyone — not just low-handicap golfers, not just members, not just people who already know golf
- Grounded: The course is described accurately, not aspirationally. "A well-maintained 9-hole public course" rather than "a premier golf experience" — because the former is true and the latter sounds like marketing
- Confident about quality: Credentials and course maintenance are stated without apology — but without bragging

**The tone IS NOT:**
- Exclusive or prestige-signaling: No "discerning golfer" language, no "award-winning experience," no language that implies the golfer needs to earn their place here
- Urgency-driven: No "book before it fills up" or "limited spots remaining" for a public golf course — it would read as desperate and be factually inaccurate
- Overly casual or colloquial: The brand is a golf club, not a sports bar. "Grab your clubs and come hang" is too casual; "the game awaits" is too formal — the middle register is "Book your round today"
- Generic service business language: No "trusted by hundreds," no "we're passionate about golf," no "your satisfaction is our priority"

**How the brand speaks about the customer:** As a golfer who deserves a good round at a fair price. Not as a beginner who needs hand-holding. Not as a prestige-seeker who needs to be impressed. Simply as someone who wants to play golf.

**How the brand speaks about itself:** Matter-of-factly. The course is what it is: a well-maintained public 9-hole course managed by a PGA professional with a professional instruction program on-site. The copy states these facts and trusts the audience to recognize their value.

**Tone in specific situations:**
- Proof statements: State the fact and let it stand — "Managed by Scott Haynes, PGA of Canada Professional" — no exclamation mark, no superlatives
- CTAs: Direct and action-specific — "Book a Tee Time," not "Get Started" or "Learn More"
- Process explanations (course specs, lesson programs): Clear and informative — assume the visitor knows what golf is but may not know this specific course
- Trust/reassurance moments: Understate rather than oversell — "No membership required. No dress code enforcement. Just golf." is more reassuring than "We welcome golfers of all levels!"

## 18. Founder / Local / Business Story Strategy

**Story role:** Supporting trust anchor — the community connection and local roots story reinforces the neighbourhood-course positioning and differentiates from corporate golf operations.

**Local angle:** Strong. "Markham's neighbourhood course" is one of the five key themes. The local roots angle — the community-feel, regulars-know-each-other atmosphere — is authentic and verifiable. It should appear on the homepage (trust bar or course overview section) and on the About page.

## 19. What the Brand Must Not Feel Like

- **Must not feel like a private members' club:** The primary audience has been made to feel unwelcome by private clubs. Any design element, copy, or structural choice that signals membership, exclusivity, or gatekeeping will drive this audience away. No "Members" nav items, no "apply for access" language, no imagery of formal dress-code golf.

- **Must not feel like a generic local business template:** The current Markham Green site fails partly because it looks like a WordPress theme with clip art. The redesign must feel considered and specific — dark green photography-led design, serif headings, real course imagery. If it looks like every other small business site, it will convert like one.

- **Must not feel cheap:** $44 green fees should feel like a good deal at a real course, not the price of a neglected park. The visual quality (typography, photography, layout) must support the "proper golf club" perception even as the copy makes accessibility explicit. Cheap design signals a cheap course.

- **Must not feel urgent or salesy:** This audience makes leisure decisions. They are not buying emergency services. Any urgency framing, countdown timers, "don't miss out" language, or "limited spots" messaging will feel out of place and reduce trust.

- **Must not feel corporate or anonymous:** Chain golf operations manage courses as venues. Markham Green's differentiator is that Scott Haynes and Greg Salazar are the names behind it — real people with real credentials. Any copy or design treatment that removes the human element and replaces it with brand-voice corporate language undermines the community-feel positioning.

- **Must not feel like Buttonville:** The competitive positioning requires Markham Green to feel clearly above the municipal-course tier — better maintained, more professionally managed, more welcoming as a specific community rather than a generic park facility.

## 22. Practical Brand Rules for Later Skills

**For `06-design-system`:**
- Dark hunter green and near-black are the primary surfaces — these signal "proper golf club" at a glance. The palette must read as quality, not as a dark color scheme applied generically. Typography weight and photography overlay must do the heavy lifting.
- Warm cream/off-white for body sections prevents the dark-heavy palette from feeling oppressive or closed-off. The alternation between dark (hero, trust bar, CTA bands) and warm light (content sections) is the visual rhythm of the "refined but welcoming" register.
- The gold/amber accent is used only as an action signal — CTA buttons, hover states, a single accent element per section at most. It must not become a dominant visual element; it is punctuation, not theme.
- Typography must communicate club quality without formality: the serif heading paired with a clean geometric sans-serif body is the combination that achieves "golf club" without "private club." Do not use display serifs that feel baroque or heavy.
- Photography is load-bearing. The design system should treat photography as a first-class visual element — full-width hero, 3-column grid section on homepage — not a decorative accent inserted into card corners.
- Course imagery must show real golfers in natural light, or empty course shots at golden hour. No stock photography of generic golfers in polos on courses that clearly aren't Markham Green.

**For copy / content stages:**
- Always lead with what the visitor gets, not what the business provides — "A real 9-hole round in Markham" before "We offer public green fees"
- State credentials completely on first mention: "Scott Haynes, PGA of Canada Professional" — not "our pro," not "our certified instructor"
- Price is stated plainly and early — never hedged, never buried. "$44 weekday green fees" not "competitively priced green fees"
- CTAs use action-specific language: "Book a Tee Time," "Contact Greg," "Call the Pro Shop" — not "Learn More," "Get Started," or "Reach Out"
- Avoid superlatives entirely — the brand is confident without needing to be the best. Let the credentials and pricing make the argument.

**For build / code stages:**
- Phone number ((905) 294-6156) must be in the sticky header as a tap-to-call link on mobile — this is a structural brand requirement, not optional
- Trust bar (Google rating + PGA credentials) is a required section immediately below the hero — it must not be movable or conditional
- Pricing table on `/course` must render cleanly on mobile as stacked tier cards — if the pricing is hard to read on mobile, the primary conversion path (casual golfer on mobile → confirms price → calls) breaks
- `siteConfig.bookingUrl` toggle must be implemented so the CTA can swap from tel: to booking URL in one config line — the brand promise is "easy to book" and this must remain true when the booking system is added

## 25. Blockers and Risks

**Blockers:**
- Photography must be reviewed before design system begins. If photos are not hero-grade, the visual direction must be replanned.

**Risks:**
- **Photography risk (critical):** If drone shots are unusable, the design system needs to compensate with stronger typographic design and more abstract visual treatment. This would push the register toward "more corporate" and away from the photography-led "warmth" — a significant brand trade-off.
- **Proof thinness:** Without confirmed Google review count and no testimonials, the trust strategy relies heavily on photography and credentials. This is workable but not ideal. Client should be encouraged to gather 2–3 quotes from past customers before launch.
- **Community feel authenticity:** The "neighbourhood course" positioning is powerful but difficult to verify from the outside. If the course has high staff turnover, an impersonal pro shop experience, or a course condition that contradicts the "well-maintained" claim, the brand promise breaks on delivery.
