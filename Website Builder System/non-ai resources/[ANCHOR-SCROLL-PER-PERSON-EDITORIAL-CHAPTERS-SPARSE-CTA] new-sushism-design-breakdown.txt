NEW SUSHISM DESIGN BREAKDOWN
Source: https://new-sushism.jp/
Analyzed: 2026-04-05
==================================================

CONTEXT
--------
New Sushism is a Tokyo-based multi-location sushi concept spotlighting innovative artisans
across Shibuya, Shinjuku, and Ebisu. This site is a strong reference for:
- Bilingual luxury hospitality/restaurant branding
- Story-first editorial design for a small, curated offering
- Japanese design sensibility applied to a Western-readable site
- "Platform for people" positioning (the brand elevates its artisans, not just the food)


1. LAYOUT STRUCTURE
-------------------
Single-page scroll with anchored section navigation. Each Tokyo location gets its own
full section — the structure mirrors a magazine editorial layout, not a product site.

Section order:
  Header Nav → Hero → About / Philosophy → Shibuya Artisan → Shinjuku Artisan
  → Ebisu Artisan → Image Slider Galleries → Footer

No sidebar, no grid of options, no comparison tables. The page moves in one direction: down.
Each section is treated as its own "chapter" with a distinct visual identity.


2. HERO SECTION FORMULA
------------------------
Structure: Philosophical statement (bilingual) + brand name as visual anchor

Japanese headline: 次世代の寿司文化を築く、革新的な職人たちに出会える場所
English parallel: "A place to meet innovative artisans shaping next-generation sushi culture"

- No price, no discount, no reservation CTA in the hero
- The hero is a cultural positioning statement, not a sales pitch
- Bold display text ("SUSHISM," "INNOVATION WHERE TRADITION") creates visual dominance
- The brand name itself functions as the headline — it's a compound concept worth explaining

Formula to copy (for cultural/hospitality brands):
  [Cultural mission statement] (bilingual if relevant) + [Brand name as visual anchor]
  → Let the photography do the rest

What this hero deliberately avoids:
  - No "Book a table" above the fold
  - No opening hours
  - No star ratings
  - All of that comes later, if at all


3. TYPOGRAPHY CHOICES
----------------------
- Typekit custom font kit (ID: awk1txa) — suggests a premium Japanese/Latin pairing, likely
  a refined Gothic (sans) for Japanese text and a display serif or geometric for Latin
- Bold/heavy weight display text for hero and section titles — creates strong visual anchors
- Light weight body copy for artisan philosophy and descriptions — maximizes contrast
- Bilingual hierarchy: Japanese primary, English secondary — or parallel, depending on section
- No all-caps abuse — uppercase is reserved for brand/section labels ("SUSHISM," "VIEW MORE")
- The mix of languages IS the typography choice — the visual rhythm of kanji + Latin letterforms
  creates texture that a monolingual site can't replicate


4. SPACING AND SECTION RHYTHM
------------------------------
- Generous whitespace between all major sections — the pacing is meditative, not urgent
- Full-width image zones alternate with contained text blocks — same editorial rhythm as a
  printed food magazine
- Navigation maintains consistent padding; CTAs are visually isolated through surrounding space
- Three artisan sections use identical internal structure (image carousel → name/age/hometown → story)
  — this creates a satisfying rhythm of repetition with variation
- "LEFT RIGHT / SCROLL DOWN" directional prompts appear at image sliders — the site teaches
  interaction rather than assuming it


5. CTA PLACEMENT
-----------------
Location              | CTA              | Purpose
--------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------
Header                | "CONTACT"        | Always accessible, never pushy
Artisan profiles      | "VIEW MORE"      | Progressive disclosure — earn the click
Artisan profiles      | Tabelog links    | Third-party reservation/rating (external trust)
Artisan profiles      | Instagram links  | Social proof / personality
Footer                | "CONTACT"        | Recovery CTA

Key pattern: Extremely minimal CTAs. The site does not ask you to do anything —
it invites you to go deeper. "VIEW MORE" is the only real internal navigation CTA.

Contact is via mailto — no embedded booking widget, no form. This is intentional:
the friction of email signals exclusivity, not accessibility.


6. TRUST-BUILDING ELEMENTS
---------------------------
- Three named, profiled artisans with: name, age, hometown, philosophy — highly personal
- Tabelog links (Japan's dominant restaurant review platform — equivalent to OpenTable + Yelp)
  provide external validation without cluttering the page
- Instagram links per artisan add personality and living proof of the food
- Founder philosophy narrative: frames the brand as a cultural movement, not a restaurant chain
- Schema.org structured data (CollectionPage, WebSite) — technical SEO credibility built in
- 12+ food photography images per artisan section — volume of imagery = depth of craft

Trust approach: identity-based, not metric-based.
No star count displayed on the homepage — the Tabelog links let visitors self-verify.


7. WHAT MAKES IT FEEL PREMIUM (NOT AI-GENERATED)
--------------------------------------------------
- Bilingual text is genuinely bilingual — not Google Translated filler; both languages carry meaning
- Artisan profiles include age and hometown — hyper-specific details that feel researched, not templated
- Image sliders with directional prompts ("LEFT RIGHT") teach interaction rather than assuming
  visitors know the UX — hospitality instinct applied to web design
- Photography is extensive and consistent across all three locations — suggests a real art director
  and photographer, not stock imagery
- The brand concept ("Sushism") is invented, specific, and unexplained at first — it earns curiosity
- No homepage hero restaurant photo clichés (no overhead food shot, no "bokeh chef" photo)
- Section divider SVGs and logo lockups suggest custom illustration work, not icon libraries
- The page structure mirrors a curated art exhibition or printed magazine — the medium matches
  the message (craft, curation, attention)


8. WHAT TO COPY FOR A CLIENT WEBSITE
--------------------------------------
- Per-person editorial profiles: name + age + hometown + philosophy — works for any human-forward brand
  (studios, agencies, practitioners, chefs, coaches)
- Bilingual or dual-register typography as a design texture — even for English-only sites,
  pairing a display language (large, abstract) with a body language (small, descriptive) creates the same rhythm
- "VIEW MORE" progressive disclosure instead of showing everything — creates depth and return visits
- External validation links (review platforms, social) embedded in profiles instead of on-page star ratings
- Anchor-scrolled single-page structure for small, curated offerings (3–5 "chapters")
- Directional interaction prompts at non-obvious UX patterns — teach the scroll, don't assume it
- Invented brand concept as the hero — "Sushism" is more memorable than "Premium Sushi Tokyo"
- Full-width image zones between constrained text sections — editorial rhythm


9. WHAT NOT TO COPY TOO LITERALLY
-----------------------------------
- The mailto contact CTA — works for exclusivity positioning, kills conversion for most clients
- The complete absence of pricing or booking above the fold — fine for a discovery-phase brand,
  frustrating for a client whose customers need practical info fast
- The Japan-specific Tabelog integration — know your equivalent platform per market
- The "no metric trust" approach — works when the photography is world-class; needs at least
  one anchor stat for clients whose visual production isn't at this level
- The invented compound brand name ("Sushism") — advise clients carefully; a concept brand
  name requires the design and content to fully support it or it reads as pretentious


TL;DR
------
New Sushism treats its website like a curated exhibition catalog — each artisan is a chapter,
each image gallery is a spread, and the brand concept is the title of the show.

The design lesson: for any client with a small, human-forward, high-craft offering,
drop the conversion playbook entirely. The job of the site is not to sell — it's to make
the visitor feel they've discovered something worth telling others about.

Discovery > conversion. Depth > breadth. Identity > metrics.
