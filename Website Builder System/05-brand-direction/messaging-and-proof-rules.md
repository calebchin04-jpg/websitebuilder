# Messaging and Proof Rules

This file defines how to build the messaging direction, brand vocabulary, proof strategy, and founder/story rules. These govern what the brand says and what makes it believable.

---

## Part 1: Messaging Direction

### What messaging direction means here

Messaging direction is not final copy. It is the strategic layer that tells later copy stages:
- What themes to repeat across the site
- What the audience needs to understand quickly
- What value frame to use
- What questions the site should answer before the visitor asks them

It answers: *What must be communicated, and in what order of importance?*

### Building the messaging direction

**Step 1 — Define the primary message**
What single thing must the audience understand about this business? This is the one claim that, if they believed it, would make them want to act.

This is not the CTA. It is the belief the CTA depends on.

Example: "This person is the careful expert who won't damage your property."
NOT: "Click here to get a free quote."

**Step 2 — Define 3–5 supporting messages**
What evidence, explanation, or proof supports the primary message? Each supporting message should make the primary message more believable or more complete.

Example supporting messages for a pressure washer:
1. "He has 47 reviews at 4.9 from people who specifically mention care and technique"
2. "He uses different equipment and pressure levels for different surfaces"
3. "He offers a free re-clean guarantee if you're not satisfied"

**Step 3 — Define the value frame**
The value frame is the lens through which the audience interprets everything they see. It tells them what kind of purchase this is and what they should evaluate it on.

Examples:
- "This is a quality-first decision, not a price-first decision"
- "This is a confidence purchase — you're paying to not worry about it going wrong"
- "This is a relationship decision — you want someone you can call again"

The value frame shapes how CTAs are written, how proof is presented, and how services are described.

## Part 2: Key Themes

Key themes are the 3–5 ideas that should appear repeatedly across the site — in headings, proof sections, about copy, and CTAs — so that the brand feels coherent and intentional rather than random.

Rules for key themes:
- Each theme should be specific to this business, not generic to its category
- Each theme should be something the audience actually cares about
- Each theme should connect to a proof asset (something that makes it believable)
- Themes should reinforce each other, not contradict

**Examples of weak vs. strong themes:**

Weak: "Quality" / "Reliability" / "Professionalism" (true of every competitor)

Strong (pressure washing):
- "Technique-first: the right tool for every surface"
- "Verified results: 47 five-star Google reviews"
- "The Marcus guarantee: free re-clean if you're not satisfied"

## Part 3: First-Impression Goals

Define what the audience should understand or feel within the first 5 seconds of landing on the site — before they read a word.

This is a brand perception goal, not a visual design instruction. It tells the design system and copy what impression to engineer.

**Format:**
> Within 5 seconds, the visitor should feel/understand: [specific belief or emotional state].

Examples:
- "This is a real expert with real results, not a random name I found online."
- "This is a serious, credible business — not a side hustle."
- "This feels like my kind of place — approachable, not intimidating."
- "This person knows exactly what they're doing and cares about doing it right."

## Part 4: Brand Vocabulary

Brand vocabulary guides later copy stages. It is not a word list to copy mechanically — it is a direction that creates verbal consistency across the site.

### Words and phrases to USE (with rationale):
List 5–10 specific words or phrase types that reinforce the brand's positioning, personality, and differentiation. Explain why each one works.

Example:
- "Careful" / "precision" / "right technique" — reinforces the technique differentiator
- "Your [surface type]" — personalizes the service, makes the audience feel seen
- "Guaranteed" — activates the guarantee differentiator
- "Results" / "before and after" — grounds the brand in proof, not claims

### Banned words and phrases:
List 5–10 words/phrases to avoid. Explain why each hurts the brand.

**Generic banned list (applies broadly):**
- "World-class" — unverifiable, sounds fake, destroys credibility
- "Best in [city]" — unverifiable superlative that every competitor also claims
- "One-stop shop" — corporate cliché with no meaning
- "Passionate about" — overused to meaninglessness in service business marketing
- "Solutions" — vague and business-jargon-y; be specific about what you actually do
- "Seamless experience" — startup language that doesn't belong in most local service contexts
- "Industry-leading" — unverifiable unless it actually is, with receipts
- "We pride ourselves on" — weak, passive, self-congratulatory framing

**Category-specific banned lists:**
Apply when relevant to the business type. For example:
- Medical: avoid "amazing results," "transform yourself," "look 10 years younger" — implies false promise
- Consultant: avoid "game-changer," "disruptive," "innovative solutions" — means nothing in this context
- Home services: avoid "professional team" when it's one person — dishonest scale framing

## Part 5: Proof Strategy

### What proof strategy means here

Proof strategy defines what evidence the brand needs in order to be believable, and in what order that evidence should be surfaced.

Proof is not decoration. It is the mechanism by which trust is transferred from claims to belief.

### Proof type inventory

Before defining the strategy, identify all available proof:

| Proof type | Available? | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Google / review platform reviews | [Y/N, count, rating] | [Strong / Adequate / Thin] |
| Testimonials (written) | [Y/N, count] | [Strong / Generic / None] |
| Before/after photography | [Y/N] | [Strong / Weak / None] |
| Case studies / named results | [Y/N] | [Strong / Partial / None] |
| Years in business | [Number] | [Strong signal if 5+] |
| Certifications / licenses | [Y/N, names] | [Relevant / Table stakes] |
| Recognizable client names | [Y/N] | [Strong if recognizable to audience] |
| Guarantee | [Y/N, type] | [Strong conversion signal] |
| Media mentions / press | [Y/N] | [Strong if credible source] |
| Founder credentials | [Y/N] | [Relevant for expertise-based businesses] |

### Proof strength assessment

After inventory, assess overall proof position:

- **Strong:** Multiple proof types with real specificity (reviews + before/after + guarantee + years)
- **Adequate:** 1–2 strong proof types, rest thin
- **Thin:** No strong proof; must rely on brand signals, process transparency, and founder story
- **None:** No proof at all — high-risk brand territory

**If proof is thin or none:** The brand direction must explicitly account for this. Proof alternatives include:
- Transparent process description (building trust through explanation)
- Founder personal story (trust through human authenticity)
- Guarantee or risk-reversal (trust through reduced perceived risk)
- Explicit credential display (trust through demonstrated qualifications)

## Part 6: Proof Hierarchy

Define which proof type should be surfaced first, and why.

**Hierarchy construction rule:** Lead with the proof type that is:
1. Strongest (most credible, most specific)
2. Most relevant to the audience's primary fear or doubt
3. Most scannable (can be understood in 2 seconds)

**Examples of proof hierarchies:**

For a pressure washer with 47 reviews:
1. Google star rating + review count (scannable, third-party verified)
2. Before/after photos (visual, immediate)
3. Guarantee badge (risk-reversal for undecided visitors)
4. Specific testimonial quotes (depth for engaged visitors)

For a consultant with no reviews but strong case studies:
1. Named case study result (specific, credible)
2. Founder credentials (years, background)
3. Process transparency (what working together looks like)
4. Generic testimonial (weakest but still useful)

## Part 7: Founder / Local / Business Story Rules

### When to use a story

Use founder/local/business story when it:
- Strengthens differentiation (e.g., founder's expertise or background directly explains why they're better)
- Builds personal trust (e.g., a solo operator where the person IS the brand)
- Provides a "why" that the audience finds meaningful (e.g., started the business after being treated poorly elsewhere)
- Creates local/community connection that competitors lack

Do NOT use story when it:
- Is purely self-congratulatory with no audience benefit
- Distracts from the primary conversion path
- Is fabricated or exaggerated
- Adds length without adding trust

### Story construction rules

A brand story that builds trust answers these questions:
1. **Who is this person?** (Real, specific, not abstract)
2. **Why do they do this work?** (Motivation that the audience can respect)
3. **What makes them the right choice?** (Story that connects to differentiation)
4. **Why should I trust them specifically?** (Proof that the story is real)

Story length direction: Most local business websites need 3–5 sentences of story, not a 500-word autobiography. The story should make the person feel real, not make the site feel like a blog.

### Local angle rules

For local businesses, "local" is only a brand asset when:
- The audience specifically prefers local over national (home services, community-based businesses, hyperlocal trust situations)
- The local connection adds warmth or accountability that a national chain lacks
- The business has visible local roots (location, community involvement, local recognition)

"Local" is NOT a differentiator if:
- Every competitor is also local
- The audience doesn't prioritize local origin in their decision
- The framing is just "your [city] [business type]" with no story or specificity
